Preface: About Deep Learning#

What is deep learning?#

Deep learning is a branch of machine learning, and machine learning is part of artificial intelligence (AI). Their relationship can be understood as follows:

  • Artificial Intelligence: Enabling machines to demonstrate capabilities that require human intelligence (such as recognizing images or understanding language)

  • Machine Learning: Enabling machines to automatically learn patterns from data rather than being explicitly programmed

  • Deep Learning: A method of machine learning that uses multi-layer neural networks

        graph TD
    AI[人工智能<br/>Artificial Intelligence] --> ML[机器学习<br/>Machine Learning]
    ML --> DL[深度学习<br/>Deep Learning]
    ML --> SL[监督学习]
    ML --> UL[无监督学习]
    DL --> CV[计算机视觉]
    DL --> NLP[自然语言处理]

Deep learning is “deep” because its network architecture has many layers. Each layer extracts features at a different level: shallow layers detect edges and textures, while deeper layers identify object parts and semantic concepts. This hierarchical feature extraction has driven breakthroughs in image, speech, and text applications.

Black boxes and alchemy#

Why call it alchemy?#

The older generation of deep learning researchers often jokingly described their work as “alchemy.” Though self-deprecating, this phrase accurately captures certain characteristics of deep learning:

  • Complex recipes: Learning rates, batch sizes, network architectures, regularization parameters… every choice can affect the outcome

  • Uncontrollable process: Running the exact same code twice can yield slightly different results (due to random initialization and data shuffling)

  • Unpredictable results: Modifications that seem theoretically sound might prove useless or even counterproductive in practice

  • Experience over theory: Success often relies on “trial and error” rather than rigorous mathematical derivations

Alchemy can still be scientific#

Although we joke about alchemy, this does not mean deep learning is pure mysticism. A good “alchemist” will:

  • Conduct controlled experiments: Change one variable while keeping others constant to observe the impact

  • Keep experiment logs: Record configurations and results for every run to establish reproducibility

  • Understand underlying principles: Know why a technique works (e.g., BatchNorm aims to mitigate internal covariate shift)

  • Tune hyperparameters systematically: Instead of random trial and error, prioritize by importance (learning rate > network architecture > optimizer > …)

A black box is not necessarily incomprehensible#

Deep learning models are often criticized as “black boxes”—inputs go in, outputs come out, with little transparency about what happens in between. In reality, however, we have an expanding set of tools to interpret models:

  • Feature visualization: Inspect which parts of an image the convolutional layers focus on

  • Attention heatmaps: See which words Transformers attend to when processing text (see Transformer：注意力的极致 for details)

  • Ablation experiments: Remove a specific component to see how much performance drops, gauging its importance

  • Adversarial example analysis: Construct targeted inputs to probe and understand the model’s decision boundaries

These methods allow us to “open” the black box. While we may not grasp every single detail, we can at least understand which factors influence the model’s decisions.

The mindset you need#

Balance theory and practice#

Deep learning is a field where theory and practice are tightly coupled. If you only know theory without coding skills, you cannot validate your ideas; if you only write code without understanding theory, you will be stuck wandering in circles.

The recommended learning approach is:

  • Understand principles first: Know why a technique works (e.g., Dropout is an approximation of ensemble learning)

  • Implement hands-on second: Reproduce it with your own code to deepen your understanding

  • Experiment last: Validate on your own datasets and observe the actual results

Build intuition#

In deep learning, intuition is sometimes more important than formulas. When you see a new architecture, being able to intuitively judge “will this design work?” is often more valuable than deriving backpropagation formulas.

Ways to build intuition:

  • Study many architectures: Learn the design philosophies and evolution of classic networks

  • Run many experiments: Tune hyperparameters yourself to feel the impact of different choices

  • Ask “why” often: Instead of just remembering “this works,” understand why it works

Remember, many “truths” in deep learning are temporary. Best practices from five years ago might be obsolete today. Only by cultivating good intuition can you stay competitive in this fast-changing field.

Stay skeptical#

The deep learning community is filled with claims:

  • “This architecture achieved SOTA on ImageNet”

  • “Our method improved over the baseline by 5%”

  • “This trick works for all tasks”

As a learner, you need to stay skeptical:

  • Question results: Is the 5% gain statistically significant? Was there test-set leakage?

  • Question conclusions: Does what worked on one dataset generalize to others?

  • Question papers: Did the authors hide negative results? Was the baseline too weak?

  • Question authority: Even if stated by leading experts, maintain independent judgment

Skepticism is not about being argumentative; it is about building truly reliable knowledge. When you can distinguish between dependable conclusions and overhyped claims, you become a qualified deep learning practitioner.

Patience and iteration#

Finally, learning deep learning requires patience. Don’t expect to fully grasp everything on the first read, and don’t be discouraged if your first experiment fails.

  • Re-read repeatedly: You might understand 30% on the first pass, 60% on the second, and 90% on the third

  • Experiment repeatedly: Hyperparameter tuning rarely succeeds on the first try; log every result and gradually approach the optimum

  • Accept failure: Many ideas ultimately prove ineffective, but that is an integral part of the learning process

Remember that even top researchers see most of their ideas fail. The difference is that they extract insights from failure and iterate quickly to the next idea.

How to use these materials#

The design of these materials follows the learning philosophy outlined above:

  1. Start from intuition: Establish intuitive understanding for every concept before diving into mathematical details

  2. Combine theory with practice: Every theoretical point is paired with code implementation and experiments

  3. Emphasize comparison and skepticism: Encourage you to question, experiment, and form your own judgment

  4. Progressive depth: Transition step-by-step from foundational concepts to cutting-edge techniques

The recommended study strategy is: read through first to build a macro view, read carefully to grasp details, and validate with hands-on experiments. If you encounter tricky spots, feel free to skip them temporarily—later sections often help clarify earlier concepts.

Reading alongside the “Fish Book”#

If you are reading Koki Saito’s Deep Learning from Scratch: Building with Python from First Principles (commonly known as the “Fish Book”), congratulations—you hold one of the most acclaimed introductory books in deep learning. These course materials do not replace the Fish Book; they are complementary.

What the Fish Book covers#

The Fish Book consists of 8 chapters, starting from Python basics and building everything from scratch using only NumPy without external libraries:

The core philosophy of the Fish Book is: “You only truly understand what you build yourself.” It forces you to write every line of code by hand, so you know exactly what is happening under the hood when PyTorch runs .backward() or nn.Conv2d.

Connecting Fish Book Ch. 1–2 with these materials: Fish Book Chapter 1 (Python/NumPy) and Chapter 2 (Perceptrons) are very basic. These materials assume readers already know Python and NumPy. If you are unfamiliar with Python, we suggest skimming Chapter 1 of the Fish Book first; if you are already comfortable with NumPy array operations, you can skip Chapters 1–2 and read starting from Chapter 3 alongside our course materials.

In a nutshell#

The Fish Book is “growing a neural network out of the soil,” while these materials are “a map to see the forest clearly from the hilltop.” Reading both gives you a clear sense of how deep the roots go and how far the branches reach.

Now that you have a preliminary understanding of deep learning, know the mindset needed, and understand how to pair these materials with classic textbooks, let’s start with math fundamentals and unveil deep learning step by step.

Happy learning and good luck with your model training!
